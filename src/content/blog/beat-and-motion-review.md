## Why am I writing this review?

**Manga by Naoki Fujita (藤田 直樹)**  
**Rating: 10/10**  
**Spoilers ahead.**

I discovered _Beat & Motion_ the way I discover most of the manga I read: through Gigguk's YouTube channel. But this one had something else going for it. Unlike most melodramatic stories about chasing your dreams, its first chapter delivers a soul-crushing reality check. It practically accuses you of being lazy while inspiring you to remember your deepest dream, get up, and fight for it.

I've spent much of my life excelling academically. But school teaches you to be book-smart while neglecting skills like making connections, negotiating, putting your work out there, and selling yourself. It's frustrating to get excellent test scores without seeing that translate into tangible success. This manga was a wake-up call for me: to stop moping around and complaining about other people's success, and start doing something about my own ambitions.

I love this manga from the bottom of my heart. I just wish I hadn't had to bid it farewell.

## Dreams don't come true because you sit around thinking about them

We've all spent hours watching TV, playing games, or scrolling through social media while thinking about how much time we've wasted instead of pursuing our dreams. And then we keep doing the same things, only now with regret. That's what makes Tatsuhiko Hirayama so painfully relatable.

When he was younger, a girl in his class mocked his work and made him feel ridiculous for taking it seriously. An early success can give you the motivation to keep going when things get difficult. Tatsuhiko got an early failure instead, and it left him afraid to show anyone his drawings.

It's only when he meets Nico that he feels inspired enough to try again. But what makes Tatsuhiko a good protagonist is that he doesn't suddenly become perfect. He overpromises on his first project. He underestimates how much work animation takes. He learns why scoping a project matters and meets people who know more than he does. Unlike other hidden-talent protagonists, Tatsuhiko makes mistakes and learns from them.

> Trying harder isn't always the answer. Sometimes you have to try differently.

When I realized I was missing a skill that didn't come naturally to me, I tended to give up rather than work on it. But it's better to confront your weaknesses, and sometimes you won't even recognize them until someone tells you that you suck at something. Is that harsh? Yes. But if we only listen to praise, how are we supposed to improve?

Think about Hajime Isayama. His rough drawings became part of what made _Attack on Titan_'s Titans so unsettling. Criticism of his art helped push him toward the horror that became central to his work.

## Talent matters. So does luck. Neither replaces the work.

One thing _Beat & Motion_ gets right is that it doesn't sell the ridiculous idea that everybody can become brilliant at anything if they just work hard enough.

Tatsuhiko tries music, and it doesn't work out. Animation is different. He has real aptitude for it, even if he's inexperienced, but he still has to develop that talent through exhausting work. Being gifted is not the same as being skilled.

Luck matters too, and I'm glad the manga admits it. Tatsuhiko's animation gets attention online, and Nico Kashiwagi—a musician he admires—contacts him about collaborating. Is that lucky? Of course. Is it unbelievable? Not at all. That's how social media can work. Meeting experienced animators at college is believable too. So is learning about competitions and putting your work in front of judges. Those are opportunities, not substitutes for ability.

The college animators, especially the constantly furious Nabari and the more measured Akame, make the professional world feel lived-in. I liked that Tatsuhiko wasn't surrounded by people whose only job was to applaud him. These artists have their own opinions, tempers, experience, and standards. Sometimes they even act as competitors.

## Nico is wonderful, and the romance actually understands ambition

I love Nico. Her design is easily the most memorable in the manga, and she's interesting well beyond being Tatsuhiko's love interest.

There's an important difference between them. Tatsuhiko is scared to show the world what he can make. Nico isn't afraid to sing or put her work out there. Her struggle is more existential: who is she as an artist, and what does her music mean to her?

I wouldn't say Nico changes nearly as much as Tatsuhiko does. Her _story_ develops, but she largely remains herself. And that's fine. Not every character needs a personality overhaul to be compelling.

Their chemistry works from the beginning. They love art, challenge each other, and share genuine romantic affection. More importantly, they don't magically drop their responsibilities whenever the plot wants a cute moment.

They're busy. Tatsuhiko has animation to finish. Nico has music to make and a career to navigate. Their planned music video keeps getting postponed. It's almost funny how long that collaboration hangs over the story, but it also says something important about their relationship.

**Love doesn't make your deadlines disappear.**

Maintaining a relationship between two ambitious people comes at a cost. Nico often seems to sacrifice more because Tatsuhiko is stuck at his drawing board. The manga doesn't pretend that support is just a matter of saying encouraging things. Sometimes it means giving up time together or accepting that your partner has chosen to work. And sometimes you have to decide whether to preserve your relationship or prioritize your career.

I don't see ambition as an excuse for neglect. I see it as a choice with consequences. _Beat & Motion_ understands that distinction, and its romance is better for it.

I only wish Nico—and especially her history with her childhood friend Miu, the singer known as Miyu—had been given more room to breathe.

## Realism is the manga's greatest strength—and eventually its weakness

I loved that the story took work seriously. Opportunities were believable, relationships took effort, and becoming an animator was an actual process rather than a montage of miraculous successes.

But somewhere around the middle, entire chapters devoted to Tatsuhiko drawing and preparing his next project started to bore me. The story needed more dramatic conflict. Not absurd twists or artificial misunderstandings, but more pressure, more clashes, more consequences.

Could time skips have helped? Yes and no. Skip too much and you miss out on seeing the grueling effort that makes his progress feel earned. Show every step and the story starts dragging.

Since we know from the start that this is the story of someone who will become a successful animator, I never minded that the destination was predictable. The interesting question is **how** he gets there. I suspect those slower chapters would have felt more rewarding if the series had been allowed to keep building on them.

Still, I loved every page of this manga.

## The ending broke my heart

I didn't know the ending was coming. It caught me completely off guard, and I cried in disbelief.

I wanted more time with these people: more of Tatsuhiko's professional development, Nico's music, their romance, the supporting cast—everything.

The ending was rushed. Tatsuhiko suddenly gets the opportunity to direct a movie, and the circumstances of that offer feel far too convenient. This is a story that spent so long showing us how success is earned, only to rush through a career milestone that deserved its own substantial journey.

That doesn't erase what the earlier chapters accomplished. They already made their point. But the conclusion doesn't live up to the standard the manga set for itself.

Nico gets an even shorter end of the stick. Her story, including her relationship with Miu, needed far more development. Of all the things lost in the rush to finish, that hurts the most.

And yet, I respect how Naoki Fujita handled the limited space. Tatsuhiko telling Nico that he'll direct a movie isn't treated as some idealistic, consequence-free victory. It's tense. They have to confront what his ambition means for their priorities and for each other.

There's something especially sad about a manga that tells you to keep chasing your dreams ending before it could fully realize its own. The characters get a happy ending, but the experience of reading it ends on a much sadder note.

## So why a 10/10?

Because I loved it. I loved it enough that I don't want to reread it anytime soon. Knowing how abruptly it ends would just make me miss it all over again.

Five years ago, I probably wouldn't have appreciated it nearly as much. I was more interested in fantasy, in stories further removed from ordinary life. Now I find something inspiring in a person learning to create, meeting people, making mistakes, and taking the risk of being seen.

You don't necessarily have to turn your dream into your career, either. You can build a career that pays for your passion. What matters is making that decision instead of letting fear make it for you.

Would I recommend it? To anyone and everyone.

It's beautiful. It's lovely. I miss it a lot.
